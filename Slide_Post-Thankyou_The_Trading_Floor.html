The mechanic you were missing
Every print has a buyer and a seller. But each side is independently opening or closing. Open×Open creates a contract (+1 OI). Open×Close transfers it (0). Close×Close extinguishes it (−1). That is why 500,000 lots of volume can move OI up, down, or not at all — and why volume and OI are different animals. The tag on each print above is what the clearinghouse derives overnight; on a live screen you would never see it.

Clearing & margin — where open interest is actually made

The trader's screen ends at the fill. This is the next room. The clearinghouse novates every trade, becomes buyer to every seller, then at the close computes one official settlement price, moves cash, and — only then — counts contracts. Open interest is a byproduct of position reports, not a market datum. It is published preliminary the next morning and finalised after.

The thing to carry into a conversation
A desk person's day is governed by the variation margin call, not by the P&L on the screen. You can be right about the trade and still get carried out because the mark moved against you before it moved for you, and the cash left the account overnight. Enron's counterparties didn't fail on a view; they failed on a margin cycle. That's the mechanism under Point C on your slide 2.

What VaR does not see
Every number above assumes you can get out. VaR is a liquidity-blind measure — it prices the move, not the exit. In a market with no inventory holders standing on the other side, the exit is the whole risk. That is the gap between a gas book and a power book of identical VaR, and no ETRM screen in the industry displays it.

The tether tape — open interest into expiry

Run a contract from listing to last trading day and watch the open interest curve. Physically-settled contracts cliff into expiry, because holding past first notice day means pipeline nominations and terminal scheduling, and almost nobody wants that. Cash-settled contracts have no cliff — there is nothing to avoid. The shape of the curve into expiry is a signature of whether a physical tether exists.

This is the one chart on this page that belongs on a slide. The cliff is a delivery-avoidance artifact: it measures how badly the marginal holder wants to not take the commodity. Its absence in ERCOT North and Phelix is not a liquidity story — those contracts ride flat through final settlement because the tether is notional. It is built from free daily OI files on four exchanges, it is falsifiable, and it puts a shape on the ≈0% in the FTS row of your Point F table.
