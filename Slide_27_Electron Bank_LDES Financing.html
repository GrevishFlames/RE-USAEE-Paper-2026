The Vault
One state of charge, three creditors

A long-duration storage asset holds a single physical inventory — its state of charge. An "electron bank" pledges that inventory three ways at once: as resource adequacy to a load-serving entity, as warehouse receipts to traders, and as borrowing-base collateral to a lender. In fair weather the claims coexist. In a dunkelflaute they collide. The priority regime — who stands senior on the same megawatt-hours — determines which financing architecture is possible at all.

Why the ordering is the whole design

Regime I (reliability first) is what the CPUC and a slice-of-day ELCC study implicitly assume: encumbered or not, the electrons answer the RA call. Receipts become interruptible, so traders discount them; the lender's collateral evaporates precisely when prices peak, so the advance rate collapses toward zero. You get accreditation but no inventory finance — the merchant-plus-RA world every grim utility LDES study already modeled.

Regime III (lender first) is what a borrowing-base revolver actually requires — a perfected security interest that survives stress. But then the RA study cannot count the encumbered floor, the slice-of-day multiplier shrinks, and the contracted revenue that anchors the term debt disappears. You get cheap working capital and no capacity revenue.

Regime II (receipts first) is the gas-storage answer: firm storage service with clear title on injected volumes, RA sold only against the uncommitted remainder, lender advancing only against receipted inventory with the receipt holders' consent — the LME warrant structure, not the CDO structure. It is the only ordering under which all three counterparties can rationally sign, and it is the ordering no electricity market currently has legal machinery to enforce. That machinery — title, seniority, and settlement on state of charge — is the electron bank. Everything else in the brainstorm is decoration.

The number the gas analogy hides

Working gas in a storage field can be a double-digit percentage of facility value, which is why inventory revolvers matter there. Here the full reservoir at the forward marker is worth a fraction of one percent of capex — stored electricity is nearly worthless as commodity. What carries value is the option it embeds: firm deliverability into a $1,000+/MWh scarcity window. The lendable object is therefore not the inventory but the seniority of a claim on state of charge during stress — which is exactly the instrument no power market can currently perfect, and exactly what an electron bank would exist to issue. The brainstorm's inventory-financing leg survives only after this substitution: warehouse receipts on electrons are scarcity options with physical settlement, not commodity warrants. That reframing is publishable; the unmodified gas analogy is not.

The Branch Network
Five vaults, one balance sheet

The Gas Bank was never the storage — it was the portfolio. An electron bank’s asset side is a geographically distributed LDES fleet managed as one inventory: SP15, NP15, Palo Verde, Mid-C, a Utah cavern. The ATM analogy says the portfolio never runs dry because branches cover each other. That is true under exactly two conditions: scarcity must be imperfectly correlated across hubs, and transmission must be deliverable when it binds. This exhibit sells firm blocks against the fleet, then sweeps the correlation of drought — and watches the diversification claim hold, thin, and fail.

The live inventory map: five storage branches wired to a common bus, each gauge showing that hub’s worst state of charge of the year and the day it lands, link width showing interface rating at the current transmission setting. At low correlation the troughs land on scattered days — each branch drains during its own local drought while the others sit full. As correlation rises the trough days converge toward the common window: the whole fleet bottoms out together, which is the failure mode a portfolio-level firmness claim must survive. Note the designed mismatch: firm load is sold where load lives (SP15, NP15) but energy lives where geology allows (Mid-C, Utah). The bank’s entire business is moving inventory across that mismatch — which is why the interfaces, not the reservoirs, are usually the balance sheet’s weakest line.

The exhibit’s thesis in one chart. At low correlation the portfolio is a bank: idle branches cover the stressed one and firmness rides near 100% whenever wires exist. As correlation rises, every branch is stressed in the same hour, exporters vanish, and the curves converge toward the standalone line — five small vaults failing simultaneously. Transmission shifts the curve but cannot repeal it: no interface rating rescues a portfolio whose droughts are one drought. Diversification that evaporates under correlation is the banking analogy’s dark twin; banks fail in correlated runs too.

JThe Portfolio UnderwritingCURRENT SETTINGS vs STANDALONE

The ATM analogy, audited

Cash is fungible across branches because moving it is nearly free. Inventory at Mid-C is not fungible with a firm obligation at SP15 — it is separated by an interface that congests precisely when scarcity prices it highest. So the honest claim is conditional: a distributed LDES fleet behaves like banked inventory if and only if scarcity is imperfectly correlated across hubs and transfer capability survives the stress hour. A dunkelflaute is, by construction, a wide-area correlated weather event — it attacks the first condition; congestion attacks the second. The firmness curve above is the audit.

This is also where the hub-siting critique lands with full weight: the value of every branch depends on the correlation structure of WECC scarcity and the topology it sits behind. Siting the bank’s branches is not a real-estate decision, it is a correlation trade — and the residual firm risk the portfolio cannot diversify away is exactly the tail the Vault’s priority-of-claims machinery on the liability side exists to allocate. The two exhibits are one balance sheet: Series No. 2 is the asset book, Series No. 1 is who eats the shortfall the asset book cannot cover.

One more audit note: the “satellite-linked” layer is not novel telemetry — SCADA and ICCP already stream state data. What is novel is a commercial entity seeing state of charge across ownerships. That visibility is the bank’s informational moat and, simultaneously, a market-power question a regulator will ask before any trader does. Say it first.
